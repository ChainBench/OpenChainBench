<Summary>
Four live benchmarks across quote APIs, effective fees, and cross-chain messaging protocols spanning 8 bridge providers and 4 messaging networks.

No single provider leads quote speed, fee efficiency, and messaging latency simultaneously. The market is structurally split by architecture.

Quote latency spans from under 100 ms (intent layers) to over 2 seconds (route-searching aggregators) on identical USDC corridors probed from 3 geographic regions. The ranking shifts by corridor: a provider that leads Sol to Base can trail on Base to Arb.

Effective fee is not the same as the advertised fee. Total cost at $300 notional (fees plus slippage plus destination gas) is the figure that actually leaves a user's wallet. The gap between intent layers and aggregators widens at small notionals.

Cross-chain messaging networks (LayerZero, Wormhole, Chainlink CCIP, Axelar) differ by a structural finality tax that a source chain choice imposes. Ethereum-source CCIP lanes average 12 to 15 minutes. BNB-source LayerZero delivers in single-digit seconds. Same protocol, 100x difference, entirely driven by source-chain finality.

Regional geography matters for quote latency but not for settlement time.
</Summary>

## Methodology

Every number in this report is derived from OpenChainBench's live Prometheus instance. Four independent harnesses run continuously:

The [bridge-quote-latency](/benchmarks/bridge-quote-latency) harness issues identical USDC quote requests against 8 bridge APIs every 5 minutes from 3 geographic regions (EU-West, US-East, Singapore), across 4 corridors (Sol to Base, Base to Arb, Arb to Sol, Arb to HyperCore) and 3 notional sizes ($5, $50, $300). Latency is wall-clock milliseconds from request dispatch to last byte received. Failed quotes are excluded from the latency histogram and counted toward a separate success rate metric.

The [bridge-fee](/benchmarks/bridge-fee) harness records total cost as a percentage of notional — quoted USD in minus quoted USD delivered, all-in (fees plus slippage plus destination gas). This definition prevents providers that bake cost into a spread from appearing at 0% on explicit-fee-only comparisons.

The [layerzero-message-latency](/benchmarks/layerzero-message-latency), [wormhole-vaa-latency](/benchmarks/wormhole-vaa-latency), [chainlink-ccip-latency](/benchmarks/chainlink-ccip-latency), and [axelar-gmp-latency](/benchmarks/axelar-gmp-latency) harnesses observe real user traffic from public scan APIs. No canary transactions are sent and no gas is paid. p50/p90/p99 computed via Prometheus `histogram_quantile` over a rolling 24-hour window.

All harness source code is open at [github.com/ChainBench/OpenChainBench](https://github.com/ChainBench/OpenChainBench/tree/main/harnesses).

## Two Layers of Cross-Chain Performance

The phrase "bridge speed" conflates two things that have almost no relationship to each other.

The first layer is **API quote latency**: how fast the bridge returns a price, a route, and an estimated output amount. This is what a wallet integration feels in its loading spinner. It is measured in milliseconds and is entirely determined by the bridge's server architecture.

The second layer is **settlement latency**: how long funds take to arrive on the destination chain after the user signs. This is measured in seconds to minutes and is determined by the source chain's finality model, the bridge's validation mechanism, and the destination chain's block time.

<Mark>These two numbers are structurally independent. The fastest quote API in this cohort and the slowest produce settlements in nearly the same time window once the transaction is signed.</Mark>

Every "fastest bridge" article on the web picks one of these two dimensions and ignores the other. This report benchmarks both separately.

The leaderboard splits cleanly along architectural lines. Intent layers and relay networks return quotes in tens of milliseconds because the API call is a thin price-discovery query against a pool of pre-positioned solvers. Route-searching aggregators return quotes in hundreds of milliseconds to over a second because each request triggers a graph traversal across their integrated bridge set.

<Mark>The fastest providers in this cohort quote 8 to 20 times faster than the slowest.</Mark> Tail latency deserves its own reading: p99 is what dictates timeout policies in production wallets. A bridge with a 90 ms p50 and a 3,500 ms p99 means one in a hundred quote requests will sit at the three-second spinner threshold under load.

## The Architecture Divide

Three pricing architectures coexist in this benchmark:

**Direct protocols** (deBridge) charge a native-token fee declared upfront in the quote. Quote speed is fast. Route optionality is absent by design.

**Aggregators** (LI.FI, Squid, Socket) route through whichever underlying bridge is cheapest or fastest at quote time. Their latency reflects route-search complexity. The value proposition is coverage and route optimization, not speed.

**Intent and relay layers** (Mobula, Relay, Across, Near Intents) bake cost into a spread on a settlement intent or optimistic fill. <Mark>Quote latency and fee structure are both determined by solver competition, not a fixed fee schedule.</Mark>

The architectural type predicts both quote latency and fee dynamics more reliably than any other variable in this dataset. An aggregator that covers 30+ underlying bridges will always trade latency for route breadth. That is not an engineering deficit — it is a harder problem solved per request.

## The True Cost of Bridging

The cost figure that matters is not the fee field in a bridge's quote object. It is quoted USD in minus quoted USD delivered. Providers that bake cost into a spread appear at 0% on explicit-fee-only comparisons. This benchmark records `bridge_cost_percent`, the all-in number, so spread-based and fee-based pricing land on the same axis.

<StatTable bench="bridge-fee" caption="Effective bridge fee at $300 USDC notional (p50, 24 h) — cross-corridor aggregate. Includes fees, slippage, and destination gas. Lower is better." />

At $300 notional, fixed costs (destination gas, base protocol fee) dominate the percentage. A bridge charging $2 in destination gas on a $300 transfer is already at 0.67% before any spread or protocol fee. <Mark>Published bridge fee tables at $10,000 notional systematically understate what retail users pay at $300.</Mark>

## The Finality Tax: Messaging Protocol Latency by Source Chain

The second half of this report covers cross-chain messaging protocols. LayerZero, Wormhole, Chainlink CCIP, and Axelar each operate differently, but all four face the same constraint: <Mark>they must wait for the source chain to finalize before safely signing an attestation that a message was sent.</Mark>

This creates the finality tax. Choosing Ethereum as a source chain imposes a 12 to 15 minute finality wait on every CCIP delivery. Choosing BNB Chain imposes a 3-second block time. Same message, same destination, different source: the difference is 200x at p50.

## LayerZero: Pre-Finality DVN Architecture

<StatTable bench="layerzero-message-latency" caption="LayerZero end-to-end message delivery latency by source chain (p50, 24 h). Source-tx to destination-tx execution. Real user traffic, not canary transactions. Lower is better." />

LayerZero's DVN (Decentralized Verifier Network) architecture signs pre-finality by default: attestations are issued as soon as the source block is observed, without waiting for Casper FFG or equivalent chain-level finality. This makes LayerZero the fastest messaging network in this benchmark on Ethereum-source lanes.

<Mark>The security trade-off is explicit: pre-finality signing means the DVN could attest a message that is later re-orged out of the source chain.</Mark> For most application-layer use cases at current Ethereum re-org rates, this risk is acceptable. For high-value settlements, CCIP's finality-first model is the more conservative choice.

BNB Chain and Solana lead LayerZero delivery because their source blocks finalize in sub-second to low-second windows. DVN verification and Executor delivery then dominate, adding typically 5 to 15 seconds. Ethereum-source lanes inherit the ~12s block-time floor before DVN work begins.

## Wormhole: Guardian Quorum and Source-Chain Floors

<StatTable bench="wormhole-vaa-latency" caption="Wormhole VAA finalization latency by source chain (p50, 24 h). Source-tx observation to Guardian signing quorum (13 of 19). Real user traffic. Lower is better." />

Wormhole Guardians (19 nodes, 13-of-19 quorum) wait for finality before attesting. The finality latency by chain maps almost exactly to VAA availability: Sui and BNB with sub-second BFT finality produce VAAs in 5 to 10 seconds; Solana's 32-slot finalized commitment floors Solana-source VAAs at 15 to 25 seconds; Ethereum-source inherits the Casper FFG two-epoch wait.

<Mark>Wormhole VAA availability is a direct function of source-chain finality.</Mark> The network adds roughly 5 to 10 seconds of overhead above that floor for Guardian signing and wormholescan indexing. Once a VAA reaches quorum, any relayer can deliver it to any destination chain.

## Chainlink CCIP: Safety Before Speed

<StatTable bench="chainlink-ccip-latency" caption="Chainlink CCIP end-to-end delivery latency by source chain (p50, 24 h). Source-tx to destination-tx execution. SUCCESS messages only. Lower is better." />

CCIP's DON commits a merkle root only after source-chain finality is confirmed — the strongest safety guarantee among the four networks. A delivery is only possible after the source block has reached economic finality, so the message cannot be invalidated by a re-org.

<Mark>Ethereum-source CCIP deliveries routinely take 12 to 15 minutes at p50.</Mark> L2s that inherit Ethereum finality (Arbitrum, Optimism) show similar floors. Sub-second BFT source chains (BNB, Solana, Avalanche) allow CCIP to commit within minutes. The source chain selection guide is simple: use BNB or Avalanche as source chain if CCIP delivery latency matters for your use case.

## Axelar: Cosmos Validator Quorum

<StatTable bench="axelar-gmp-latency" caption="Axelar GMP end-to-end delivery latency by source chain (p50, 24 h). Source-tx to destination execution. Measured via AxelarScan API on real traffic. Lower is better." />

Axelar processes messages through its own Cosmos-SDK chain: source-chain validators observe the message, an Axelar validator quorum confirms it, and a gateway contract on the destination executes it. The Axelar chain has a 6-second block time, which floors all per-stage latencies at 6-second increments.

The two stages AxelarScan exposes are `call_confirm` (source tx to Axelar quorum) and `total` (source tx to destination execution). Applications built on Axelar (Squid Router, ITS token bridges) are the primary traffic this benchmark observes — their presence in both the quote-latency bench and this GMP bench illustrates how aggregator quote speed and settlement speed are decoupled in practice.

## Decision Framework

<UseCase label="Wallet or dApp integrating a swap UI">
Quote latency is the primary UX variable. Use the per-region tables above (EU-West, US-East, Singapore) to find the current leader for your users' geography, then cross-reference with the per-corridor tables for the specific routes your wallet offers. The aggregate leader may trail on a specific corridor. Parallelize quote requests across 2 to 3 providers and display the fastest valid response. The success rate column is the secondary filter: a bridge quoting successfully 30% of the time is not a reliable default regardless of its latency rank on successful calls.
</UseCase>

<UseCase label="High-volume or recurring cross-chain transfers">
Effective fee at your typical notional is the primary variable. The per-corridor cost tables surface where each provider is structurally cheap and where it is not. The aggregate leaderboard at $300 and $10,000 are different rankings; verify at the size that matches your transfers. On the Arb to HyperCore corridor, expect wider spreads until solver inventory deepens.
</UseCase>

<UseCase label="DeFi protocol requiring cross-chain message delivery">
Source chain choice is the most important decision for messaging latency. If you need delivery in under 60 seconds, Ethereum as source chain is structurally incompatible with finality-first protocols (CCIP, Wormhole). LayerZero with pre-finality DVN signing is the fastest option on Ethereum-source lanes at the cost of a finality trade-off. If your protocol requires finality guarantees, choose a source chain with sub-second BFT finality (BNB, Solana, Avalanche) regardless of which messaging network you use.
</UseCase>

<UseCase label="Institutional or high-value asset bridge">
Chainlink CCIP's finality-first model is the conservative choice. No re-org can invalidate a committed CCIP message. Accept the 12 to 15 minute delivery time on Ethereum-source lanes as a property of the security guarantee. For use cases where finality is required but 15 minutes is too slow, use BNB Chain or Avalanche as source chain to cut delivery to 2 to 5 minutes while keeping the same CCIP security model.
</UseCase>

<UseCase label="Cross-chain application requiring broad corridor coverage">
Aggregators (LI.FI, Socket, Squid) cover more source and destination chain combinations than direct protocols or single-network intent layers. Check the per-corridor success rate column in the quote-latency tables: a provider that shows as available in the aggregate may return `unsupported` on the specific corridor you need. Their broader coverage comes with higher quote latency and, on uncongested corridors, slightly higher effective fees.
</UseCase>

## Conclusion

The clearest pattern across all four bridge benchmarks is that the market is split along two independent axes: the API layer and the settlement layer.

At the API layer, intent-based architectures lead on quote speed and on effective cost for liquid corridors. The per-corridor tables make this concrete: the intent-vs-aggregator gap is largest on Base to Arbitrum where solver competition is deepest, and smallest on the newer HyperCore corridor where all providers face thin inventory.

At the settlement layer, the source chain is the dominant variable, not the protocol. A builder who switches from LayerZero to CCIP on an Ethereum-source lane does not get faster delivery; they get a stronger finality guarantee at the cost of 10 additional minutes. A builder who switches the source chain from Ethereum to BNB while keeping CCIP cuts delivery from 15 minutes to 3 minutes. <Mark>Source chain selection is a larger lever on settlement latency than messaging protocol selection.</Mark>

No provider in this cohort leads all four dimensions. Production applications will need at least two providers: one intent layer for speed and cost on liquid corridors, one aggregator as fallback for niche corridors. The messaging protocol choice should follow the source chain constraint, not precede it.
