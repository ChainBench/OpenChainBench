## Speed against price

No provider is both fast and cheap. The two cheapest, BlockMachine and Dwellir, are the two slowest; the fastest, Quicknode, bills $1,049 for 100M dapp requests. Chainstack and GetBlock sit between: Chainstack is cheaper and has the lower median, GetBlock the tighter p99.

## WebSocket push

Quicknode delivers new blocks first in 13 of the 18 cells the race can report, on Base, BNB Chain, Polygon and Arc by a wide margin. The exceptions are Ethereum, where GetBlock's regional sockets win from US East (94%) and Singapore (61%), and HyperEVM, where Dwellir leads two regions and GetBlock the third.

<WsFirstChart />

- **Chainstack is first on almost nothing on Polygon**, between 0.0% and 0.4% in the three regions. It also dropped the most connections of the four racing providers: 9 in 7 days, against 5 for GetBlock, one for Dwellir and none for Quicknode.
- **Ethereum is the one chain where the socket and the HTTP endpoint disagree by provider.** GetBlock takes 94% of US East pushes and 61% of Singapore while never holding the fastest HTTP median anywhere.
- **HyperEVM has no leader that holds across regions.** Dwellir takes Singapore with 91% and US East with 67%, but only 2% of EU West, where GetBlock leads with 61%. Pick on the region you serve from, not on the chain.

Alchemy left the WebSocket race on 15 October, because it bills each pushed message and the key, a Free plan one then, could not carry it. Its sockets came back up on 12 November, on all seven chains and all three regions, and it is not in the table above: over the seven day window it has 1,307 readings against 20,160 for each of the four established providers, so it has been on the track for about a sixteenth of the race. It is not losing, it was not there. It enters the ranking once it has a full window. BlockMachine has not been added yet. Arbitrum and Robinhood were taken out of the race on 17 October: they produced 73% of all pushes, and one provider's monthly message limit had been reached.

Solana came back to the race on 5 November and its three cells are still withheld, for a reason that has nothing to do with the providers. Over these 7 days Quicknode's Solana socket held its subscription for 16.6% of the clock against 8.3% for Chainstack, GetBlock and FluxRPC, which is to say it was listening for twice as long. The gap is ours and not theirs: held open by hand from a laptop, GetBlock's Solana socket ran 150 seconds and 716 pushes without a single close, so nothing is being cut at their end. A race whose competitors are not on the track for the same share of it cannot be read as a race whichever way the numbers fall, so those three cells stay out until the probe holds them open evenly. FluxRPC therefore has no published cell here at all, Solana being the only chain it serves.

## What the methods show

The first version of this report measured one cheap call, and said so in its
method: a heavy method or another location can change the order. A reviewer
made the same point and asked for five things. Each has a subsection below:
mix tip-of-chain and archive calls, add one heavy `eth_call`, split
`eth_getLogs` into its three filter shapes, treat Solana separately against
Solana-native providers, and put an API support matrix next to the latency.

All five are answered with measurements rather than with documentation. Nine
methods are now timed on the same six keys, from a one-read `eth_call` to a
trace, and no published cell rests on fewer than 2,839 readings over its 24
hour window. The Beacon API is the one measurement taken by hand and dated
rather than put on a board, for the reason given in its own subsection.

The short version, before the detail: the order changes with the method,
twice over. The provider with the fastest cheap call cannot serve two of the
five columns it is ranked in, and the provider that is fourth on that call is
first on both of the expensive ones.

### The ranking on a cheap call is not the ranking on an expensive one

Every provider above was ranked on `eth_getBlockByNumber`, which costs a node
almost nothing. We now time nine methods on the same keys, from the cheapest
read to a trace, and the ranking is not the same twice.

<MethodSpreadChart />

Read the lines rather than the rows: every crossing is two providers swapping
places when the method changes.

| Provider | Cheap call | Block receipts | Archive, 5M back | trace_block | debug_traceTransaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | 4.9 ms | 25.9 ms | not served | not served | 23.6 ms |
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | 7.0 ms | 31.2 ms | 32.9 ms | 27.3 ms | 84.0 ms |
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | 8.5 ms | 35.2 ms | 18.0 ms | 46.5 ms | 20.2 ms |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | 9.5 ms | 35.4 ms | 44.0 ms | 116 ms | 45.4 ms |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | 21.3 ms | 66.7 ms | 236 ms | 947 ms | 186 ms |
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | 163 ms | 200 ms | 193 ms | 280 ms | 197 ms |

Ethereum, medians over 24 hours, pooled across the three probe regions, and
no cell rests on fewer than 2,839 readings. The chart above takes a region
selector because the ordering is not the same from every continent.

Medians rather than means, and the difference is not cosmetic. On the
archive read from US East the mean puts Chainstack at 222.9 ms against a
median of 31.7, because one reading in the window took two seconds. A lane
that samples four times a day will keep producing those, and the mean turns
each one into a published claim that a provider is slow.

Four things in that table are invisible on the cheap call alone.

GetBlock wins the cheap call and cannot answer two of the five columns. It is
the fastest provider in the cohort at 4.9 ms and serves neither the archive
read nor `trace_block` on Ethereum. A provider that is fast and cannot answer
the question you have is not fast for you, which is the whole reason the
support matrix below sits next to the latency.

Alchemy is fourth on the cheap call and first on the two expensive ones. Its
archive read is 18.0 ms against 32.9 for Quicknode and 236 for Dwellir, and
its `debug_traceTransaction` is 20.2 ms against Quicknode's 84.0. Rank the
cohort on the call that costs a node nothing and you get the opposite of the
order you need for an indexer.

The two trace calls do not agree with each other. Quicknode is second on
`trace_block` at 27.3 ms and fifth on `debug_traceTransaction` at 84.0;
GetBlock serves the second at 23.6 ms and not the first at all. They live in
different client namespaces and a provider can be good at one and absent on
the other, so "supports tracing" is not a property a single column can hold.

Dwellir multiplies by eleven between its cheapest and its dearest. It answers
a tip-of-chain read in 21.3 ms and `trace_block` in 947 ms, which is the
difference between serving from cache and reaching into history.

### The compute term, and why we do not rank inside it

The compute probe is a Multicall3 `aggregate3` of fifty `getEthBalance`
sub-calls, each a separate descent into the state trie, sent back to back
with a one-call version of the identical request on the same pooled
connection. The difference between the two is what the extra forty-nine
reads cost.

It separates one provider and not the rest. BlockMachine reads 19.3 ms.
Alchemy, Quicknode, GetBlock, Dwellir and Chainstack all land between 2.0 and
4.8 ms, and we decline to order that band, for a reason worth stating
precisely. Across three readings, one on 3 November and two on the 4th,
Chainstack went 2.5, 4.2, 4.8 ms and Dwellir 2.0, 3.3, 4.1. Four of the six
moved upward. The lane
started on 3 November and writes about thirty times a day, so the 24 hour
window only filled yesterday, and part of that climb is the window filling
rather than the providers changing. With four days of history we cannot
separate the two. One provider is an order of magnitude above the others; the
others are, on this evidence, the same.

The figure is also not a clock on the node alone, and the spread between
regions is how you can tell. GetBlock reads 1.2 ms from US East and 8.3 ms
from EU West, Quicknode 3.8 ms from US East and 4.9 ms from Singapore. Both
legs ride the same socket in the same pass, so the round trip itself cancels,
but the fifty-read response is about 16 KB against 486 bytes for the one-read
version, and those 15 KB still cross the wire. Read it as the cost of asking
for more work, transfer included.

A value at or below zero is withheld rather than published. A fifty-read call
cannot be faster than a one-read call, so a negative difference is noise, and
on a lower-is-better view it would otherwise sort first.

Side by side, the two legs look like this:

| Provider | One read | Fifty reads |
|---|---|---|
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | 4.9 ms | 6.4 ms |
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | 7.0 ms | 9.0 ms |
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | 8.5 ms | 11.7 ms |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | 9.5 ms | 11.5 ms |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | 21.3 ms | 29.6 ms |
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | 163 ms | 167 ms |

The reviewer's expectation was that a few expensive calls would separate
providers better than many light ones. On this workload they do not, and that
is worth reporting as a result rather than hiding as a non-finding: forty-nine
extra trie descents add between 1.5 and 8.3 ms for five of the six, which is
smaller than the gap between regions for the same provider. BlockMachine is
the exception in both directions, 163 ms before the work starts and only 4 ms
added by it.

### eth_getLogs is three different questions

Asked as one flag, every provider passes: all six serve all three filter
shapes on every chain we ask. Split by what each shape costs, they separate,
and for two providers the shape matters more than the provider does.

| Provider | Address filter | Topic filter | No filter |
|---|---|---|---|
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | 16.2 ms | 21.0 ms | 32.8 ms |
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | 18.2 ms | 56.5 ms | 61.1 ms |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | 23.3 ms | 45.9 ms | 74.4 ms |
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | 27.1 ms | 28.9 ms | 47.3 ms |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | 189 ms | 918 ms | 1,076 ms |
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | 193 ms | 241 ms | 239 ms |

Ethereum, medians over 24 hours across the three regions, five blocks per
query so the width is not what is being measured here.

A topic filter costs Dwellir 4.9 times what an address filter costs, GetBlock
3.1 times and Chainstack 2.0, while Quicknode charges 1.1 and BlockMachine
1.3. That is an indexing choice showing through: an address is a cheap
secondary index on every client, topics are not indexed the same way
everywhere, and a provider that has not built for them answers the same
question five times slower.

The unfiltered form is the dearest for everyone, which is expected, but by
how much is not: 1.2 times the address figure at BlockMachine against 5.7 at
Dwellir. If your indexer filters on topics rather than addresses, the first
column in this table is the wrong one to choose on.

Width is a separate question from shape, and it has a separate answer. The
ladder climbs 100, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 10,000, 100,000 and
1,000,000 blocks and stops at the first refusal, so the figure is the widest
window accepted, on Ethereum from US East:

| Provider | Address filter | Topic filter |
|---|---|---|
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | over 1,000,000 blocks | over 1,000,000 |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | 10,000 blocks | 10,000 |
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | 500 | 500 |

Alchemy's row says "over" because the ladder ran out before the provider
did: every rung passed, including a million blocks, so that figure is a
floor and not a guard we found. Among the five whose guard did trip, the
spread is twentyfold, and it is the number that decides how fast an indexer
can backfill. A provider that admits 500 blocks needs twenty times the round
trips of one that admits 10,000 for the same range.

Two cautions on reading this table. The filters are built to match nothing,
so what it measures is the static guard that trips before any work, not how
much data will actually come back: asked for a real contract's logs over
5,000 blocks, Alchemy refuses on response size rather than on range. And the
guard can be regional. GetBlock admits 10,000 blocks from EU West against
the 1,000 above, which is the only case in the cohort where the same plan
answers this question differently depending on where the caller sits.

GetBlock is the sharpest case. It holds the fastest cheap call in the report

of its seven chains, and two of the six are at half or below. If your work
depends on that call, the chain you need it on decides your provider before
latency does.

A cell counts a chain as not served when the provider refused and also when
we could not classify the answer, which is the conservative reading. The
measurement date and the probe cadence are in the method section.

### Trace is where the cohort separates

Of the seven methods we ask on every chain, five are served nearly
everywhere. `trace_block` is the one that disagrees:

| Provider | Methods served | trace_block |
|---|---|---|
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | 95.9% | 71.4% of chains |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | 94.6% | 62.5% |
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | 94.6% | 62.5% |
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | 91.1% | 50.0% |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | 89.3% | 50.0% |
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | 76.8% | 25.0% |

`trace_block` and `debug_traceTransaction` sit in different client
namespaces, so a provider can serve one and refuse the other on the same
chain, and several do. A documentation page that says tracing is supported
does not tell you which one you get, or on which chains.

Alchemy is in that table for the first time, and the reason it was missing
until 10 November is worth stating plainly. Our key was a Free plan one and
it reached 42 of the 56 cells, where four of the others reach all 56. On
every cell we could ask it answered, so the gap was reach and never refusal.
Alchemy upgraded that account to an Enterprise trial on 10 November 2026 and
the row above is measured on it: 53 cells of 56, and `trace_block` on five
chains of eight, which ties Chainstack.

The three cells it still does not serve are not a plan gate. Asked for
`trace_block` on Arbitrum, HyperEVM or Robinhood Chain the endpoint answers
that the method is not available on that network, and Arbitrum's
`arbtrace_block` exists but serves only pre-Nitro blocks. That is a
per-network absence, the same kind that puts GetBlock at 25%.

One detail of our own instrument belongs here, because it moved a number.
Those three refusals are explicit, but the probe files them as unclassified
rather than as refusals, so they are missing from the matrix instead of
sitting in it as zeros. Divide by what answered rather than by what was
asked and Alchemy reads 53 of 53, a tidy 100% that would make it the only
provider serving `trace_block` everywhere. The figures above divide by what
was asked. The classifier matches the phrase "not available on this network"
and Alchemy writes "not available on the ARB_MAINNET", which is the whole
of the bug.

The plan showed itself once, clearly. Success on Ethereum held at 100% to 23
October, fell to 80% on the 24th, sat at 22% from the 25th to the 31st, and
returned to 100% on 2 November. A seven day window at a fifth of the normal
answer rate that ends at the month boundary is a monthly quota, not an
outage, and it is the one thing a free key will always eventually measure
instead of the provider. A blank on these boards means we could not ask, and
never that the provider declined.

### Solana: the v2 call almost nobody serves

`getProgramAccounts` is the Solana call that separates providers, because the
cost depends entirely on which program you ask about. We ask it three ways:
against the SPL Token program with a size filter, against the Memo program
which has a small account population, and in its paginated `getProgramAccountsV2`
form.

We now hold a Solana key for six providers, and the small-program form is
served by all six. The paginated v2 form is served by two, Alchemy and
Helius, and refused outright by three: Chainstack, GetBlock and Quicknode.
FluxRPC is the sixth and it carries no verdict rather than a refusal, because
its answer to that call could not be classified either way. For anyone
planning around the v2 surface that split is the number that matters, and it
is not in any documentation table we could find.

Alchemy only joined that pair on 10 November, with the Enterprise trial. It
is worth naming the refusals rather than counting the servers, because the
count is what went wrong before: a sentence built on "one of four" survived
into production reading "nobody serves it" when the cohort changed underneath
it.

The reviewer named two Solana-native providers to compare against. Helius has
been in the cohort since 30 October on a Free plan key. FluxRPC joined on 5
November, on an account FluxRPC provided, and it is the most regional of the
six: 1.8 ms at the median from EU West, within a hair of Quicknode's 1.6, and
no Singapore endpoint at all. It is also the only one of the six under 100%
on success over these seven days, at 93.3%, and it dropped 59 WebSocket
connections where no other provider passed nine. Triton is not in the
cohort and cannot be: it has no free tier, and this report only measures keys
we actually hold. So the Solana comparison is two native providers against
four generalists, which is closer to what was asked for than the one against
three this report carried in October.

### The Beacon API, measured off the boards

The reviewer also asked about the Beacon API and archive blob sidecars. They
are absent from the matrix above and present here instead, as a dated check
rather than a live column, for a reason given at the end.

The Beacon API is the consensus layer's REST surface, not JSON-RPC, so it
answers on a different base URL from the one a provider gives you for an RPC
endpoint. Four of the six serve it:

| Provider | Beacon API | Consensus client | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| <L s="quicknode">Quicknode</L> | yes | Lighthouse v8.2.3 | same host as the RPC endpoint |
| <L s="chainstack">Chainstack</L> | yes | Lighthouse v8.3.1 | same host |
| <L s="dwellir">Dwellir</L> | yes | Caplin v3.8.1 | an unlisted host, given on request |
| <L s="alchemy">Alchemy</L> | yes | Lighthouse v8.2.2 | a separate host, on the seven-chain key |
| <L s="getblock">GetBlock</L> | no | | documented as a dedicated-node add-on |
| <L s="blockmachine">BlockMachine</L> | no | | JSON-RPC only, no consensus layer |

The first three were re-checked on 4 November 2026. Alchemy's figure is from
2 November: the beacon network is currently switched off on our own
application, so its 403 today is our configuration and not a refusal, and we
cannot re-measure it until we turn it back on.

Retention is where we expected to find a difference, and there is none.
A consensus node keeps blob sidecars for about eighteen days by default, so
anything past that is a product decision. We asked all three for the same
four slots, at roughly thirty days, six months, one year and two years back,
each chosen because it carries blobs. Every one came back served, with
responses identical to the byte. All three therefore keep blob data about
forty times longer than the client default, and none of them says so:
Chainstack's own guide still pins retention at the eighteen day default while
its endpoint returns a two year old sidecar.

If you are indexing rollup data, that is the useful answer: choose between
these three on something else, because on blob retention they are the same.

One measurement note, because getting it wrong produces a false claim about a
provider. Not every slot carries a blob transaction, so asking for a sidecar
at an arbitrary slot can return HTTP 200 with an empty list, which means that
slot had none rather than that the provider discarded it. Only a 404 is a
refusal. A first pass sampled one slot per depth, read the empty answers as
discarded data, and would have published a retention cliff that does not
exist.

This stays a dated check rather than a board column on purpose. The figures
do not move: a retention policy is a product decision, not a latency, and
probing four hosts on a schedule to watch a number stay still would spend
request budget for nothing. We re-measure it when the report is revised.

Between the cheapest and the dearest of the six, the monthly bill differs by 5.4x at 1M requests and by 12.6x at 1B. Price and speed run in opposite directions: Quicknode is the second dearest from 100M up.

Across the full 19-provider pricing cohort, BlockMachine is the cheapest dapp, indexer and archive option at 10M, 100M and 1B requests. BlockPI undercuts it in between (dapp at 50M, $108) and on simple reads at 10M ($24.50 against $25). On trace, Dwellir ties Chainstack and dRPC at $49 for 10M and is cheapest alone from just above that: $49 up to 20M, $299 at 100M. On Solana, Syndica is cheapest from 100M ($199).

Four mechanics explain most of these numbers:

- **Unit weights, not headline prices.** Dwellir and BlockMachine bill one unit per response, archive included. Alchemy charges 26 CU for an `eth_call`, so a dapp request averages 23.9 CU. Quicknode charges 20 credits for most Ethereum calls, 40 for traces and `eth_sendRawTransaction`, and 30 on Solana.
- **Trace reverses BlockMachine's lead.** It meters `debug_traceTransaction` at 25 Request Units and `trace_block` at 50: $429 for 10M trace requests, against $49 at Dwellir or Chainstack.
- **Chainstack moves in steps.** $49 includes 20M Request Units, exactly 10M archive requests at 2 RU each. At 10.1M the bill is $52, at 11M $79, at 15M $199.
- **Throughput caps come before price limits.** Alchemy pay-as-you-go stops at 30,000 CU a second, about 128M trace requests a month, and BlockMachine at 4,167 RU a second, about 428M. Past those volumes neither can serve the trace mix at any price.

These are list prices, not invoices. Alchemy and Quicknode return no usage header, so we cannot reconcile their billed units with our own traffic.

## Free plans

Free plans cover a prototype, not a product. Among the six, only Chainstack's free plan carries more than 1.5M dapp requests a month; dRPC, outside our latency cohort, carries the most at 10.5M.

Quicknode offers a one-month trial that does not renew, so it has no ranked free plan. Dwellir's free plan gives 100,000 responses a day but excludes `eth_getLogs`, which the dapp mix calls.

## Method

| Measurement | How | Window |
|---|---|---|
| HTTP latency | `eth_getBlockByNumber` (`getSlot` on Solana) every 120 s per provider, chain and region. Calls alternate between a pooled connection (warm) and a new DNS, TCP and TLS connection (cold). Each answer is classed ok, http_err, jsonrpc_err, stale or timeout. | 21 Oct 2026 13:22 to 28 Oct 2026 13:22 UTC |
| WebSocket push | One `eth_subscribe(newHeads)` socket per provider and region, ten minutes in every hour. Each block is scored against the first provider to push it in that region. GetBlock issues one socket per chain and region and has none for HyperEVM in US East and Singapore or for Arc in Singapore. | Same |
| API capability | Seven methods asked once per provider, chain and region every six hours from three regions: the three `eth_getLogs` filter shapes, `debug_traceTransaction`, `trace_block`, `eth_getBlockReceipts`, and a balance read five million blocks behind the head. A cell is published only when the provider answered; a blank means we could not ask. | 24 h to 4 Nov 2026 |
| Compute work | A Multicall3 `aggregate3` of fifty `getEthBalance` sub-calls minus a one-call version of the same request, both primed and sent back to back on one pooled connection, every two hours from three regions. Values at or below zero are withheld, and the published figure averages the regions. | Same |
| eth_getLogs window | A ladder of 100 to 1,000,000 blocks per filter shape, stopping at the first refusal. Filters are built to match nothing, so the figure is the static width guard rather than a data volume. One region, US East. | Same |
| Method latency | The same archive read and address-filtered `eth_getLogs` as the capability probe, timed rather than only classed, and published as a mean over the window. A provider that declines the call has no cell: there is no latency for a request that was refused. | Same |
| Cost | Each provider's published unit weights applied to fixed method mixes, cheapest plan that can serve the volume, throughput caps enforced. | Catalogue of 22 Oct 2026 |
| Free plans | Each renewing free allowance converted into requests of the same mixes. | Same |

Probes run from US East (Virginia), EU West (Amsterdam) and Singapore. Latency is the 7-day percentile of raw probe samples, steadier than the 24-hour figure on the bench pages. Validation Cloud left the cohort on 18 October after its key returned 401 on every call.

The latency ranking above is built on one cheap call. A heavy method does change the order, which is why the compute, log window and capability probes were added on 30 October 2026 and have their own section; another location still can. Prices exclude negotiated discounts, and dRPC prices are an upper bound because it routes to operators who may charge less.
